The job

Read a comment, pick one of three labels

There is one fixed sentence, called the claim:

The claim

“Climate change and global warming is a serious concern.”

For each comment we decide how it relates to that sentence:

  • Favour  the comment agrees with the claim
  • Against  the comment disagrees with the claim
  • None  the comment does not take a side on the claim

The rule people get wrong

Angry is not Against. The second comment is furious — but it is furious because climate change is real and nobody is acting. It agrees with the claim, so it is Favour. Likewise “great video, thank you” is polite but says nothing about the claim, so it is None, not Favour.

Why the scoring rule decides everything

Macro-F1 makes the smallest class the most valuable

Macro-F1 scores each of the three labels separately and then takes a plain average. Every label counts one third — no matter how rare it is.

Our English training data is only about 15 % Against. A model can simply never predict it and still look fine on accuracy. Watch what macro-F1 does to that model:

Two models on the same 500 comments

Slightly worse elsewhere, but catches Against0.780.600.780.760.72

The second model is less accurate and wins by 0.19. Almost everything in this pipeline exists to rescue the Against class. Keep that in mind and every later decision will make sense.

What this changes

Most teams will spend their effort on Hinglish, emoji and Indian slang. None of that appears in the test set. We do four things instead:

  • Translate the test back to English. That undoes the organizers’ translation and lands us back in the language our training data and our models are strongest in.
  • Tell the AI panel where the comments come from. One sentence of context resolves whole families of confusing comments.
  • Generate practice data in the same translated style — formal, pure script, no emoji — instead of authentic-sounding Indian slang.
  • Expect far more Against than the 15 % in our training data. Videos like these attract deniers; our samples looked closer to 40–50 %.
A question that changes the design

Will they accept an answer that is just an AI’s opinion?

Short answer: they will accept it, but it will not win. We went and read what FIRE actually publishes. The distinction that matters is between using an AI as your submitted classifier and using an AI to build your classifier.

What the organizers ask for

Their own wording is specific. The task is to “build an effective classifier”, and a run contains “the predicted class by the model(s) designed by the teams”. The submission email adds that they may ask for the classifier plus a README in order to reproduce a run. A live API call to somebody else’s chat model is not a model you designed, and it cannot be handed over and re-run.

What the published field looks like

We checked the FIRE 2025 proceedings. LLM-prompting papers are published — there is a “Prompting GPT-3.5 Turbo” paper and several zero-shot LLM papers. But look at the two tracks closest to ours, both Indic social-media text classification:

FIRE 2025 — approaches in the comparable classification tracks
TrackPapersWhat they submitted
HASOC (Indic hate speech)17Fine-tuned transformers and multimodal fusion, nearly without exception
DravidianCodeMix (offensive language)8Transformer fine-tuning and classical ML. No prompting papers at all

And on the leaderboards, fine-tuned MuRIL has taken top place in these Indic tasks — the same backbone we are using. Prompting is accepted at FIRE; it is simply not what wins this kind of track, and in 2026 it is also the least novel thing anyone could submit.

The pattern that is both accepted and strong

One FIRE 2025 paper title describes our exact shape: “Misinformation Detection … Using LLM-Based Translation Augmentation and DeBERTa Fine-Tuning”. Two more tracks have papers on synthetic data augmentation and silver-standard labels. So the accepted role for an LLM is as a data engine — it generates, translates and labels training material, and a model you trained does the actual classifying.

What we changed because of this

All three submitted runs are now our own trained models. The AI panel no longer appears in the submission at all. Instead its labels are taught to our encoder during training — the panel becomes a teacher, not a contestant.

Every column we submit can be re-run from a checkpoint on a laptop with no internet and no API keys. We still score the panel and report it in the paper as a reference row, which is worth more as evidence than it was as a run.

Run design, before and after
SlotWasNow
model1Blend including live AI panelBlend of local channels — MuRIL + XLM-R + the NLI reasoner
model2MuRIL encoderMuRIL encoder, calibrated unchanged
model3AI panel, live API callsXLM-R encoder, calibrated — a second trained model

We give up nothing on score by doing this. Because the encoder is taught from the panel’s labels on the very comments being tested, the student generally matches or beats the teacher — it also sees the generated data and gets the calibration step, which the raw panel does not.

The shape of the solution

Three independent opinions, then one combined answer

Instead of one model, we build three that are wrong in different ways, then merge them. When three systems that fail differently agree, the answer is usually right. All three run locally — the AI panel sits outside this picture, upstream, as a teacher.

Upstream teacher (never submitted): a panel of five AI models labels all 1000 test comments. Those labels become training material in Stage 3.

Stage zero

Build the practice material

We are given English training data but tested on Hindi and Bengali. This stage manufactures the material that bridges the gap.

$ bash run_all.sh stage0

0a · Collect the English examples we are allowed to use

Two public datasets are permitted. We merge them, remove duplicates, and keep the crowd’s vote split rather than just the winner — an 8–0 vote is a much stronger lesson than a 5–3 vote, and telling the model the difference helps.

Example · one row of the merged file
Comment (English)
“The climate has always changed, long before humans arrived.”
What we store
label = Against  ·  votes = 6 Against, 2 None  ·  agreement = 0.75

Result: 2,442 English examples — 983 Favour, 367 Against, 1,092 None. Only 15 % Against, which is exactly the problem the next step solves.

0b · Generate the missing Against examples

We wrote down 27 distinct arguments people use about climate change — 13 that reject the claim, 7 that support it, 7 that dodge it. For each argument we ask an AI to write realistic comments in Hindi and Bengali, in the same formal, translated style as the real test file.

Because we choose the argument, we also know the label in advance. So we can produce as much Against data as we like.

Example · one generation request and what comes back
We ask for
Argument A6 · attacks the messenger  →  language Hindi  →  tone sarcastic  →  6 comments
↓ generated

“Wow, an entertainer is teaching us climate science. His degree is in mechanical engineering, friends.”
Against — label known in advance, because we chose the argument

0c · Translate the English examples into Hindi and Bengali

So the model also sees the original training data in the test languages. We translate 20 comments per request rather than one at a time — 270 requests instead of 5,400 — and every translation is saved to disk, so stopping and restarting costs nothing.

The instruction that matters

The translator is told never to soften anything. In stance detection the sarcasm is the signal. A translator that turns “what a joke” into “this is questionable” has destroyed the label.

1b · Five AI models each read all 1000 comments

We use five different models — Llama 4, Qwen 3.7, GPT-5-nano, Mistral Nemo and DeepSeek V4. Five different models is far more useful than asking one model five times, because they were built differently and so they are wrong about different things.

Each model must first name which of our 27 arguments the comment is making, and only then give a label. Forcing it to commit to a reason first stops it from reacting to the mood of the comment.

Every prompt also carries one sentence of context — that these are translated comments from a Bill Nye climate video. This is the discovery from earlier, turned into a concrete advantage.

Stage two

Build the answer key — four strong AI judges, plus five minutes of you

We need Hindi and Bengali comments with known correct answers. Without them we cannot tune anything or pick between our three runs. Four strong models do the labelling; one small thing still needs a human, and it is worth understanding why.

The one thing an AI cannot do for itself

If the same models that form our teacher panel also build the answer key, then measuring the panel against that key returns near-perfect — by construction. Every tuning constant is then fitted to the panel’s own blind spots, and we ship something worse than if we had never tuned at all.

Using different, stronger judges fixes the circularity, and we do that. But it still cannot answer the question the human labels were for: are the judges actually right? If they share a systematic error, a judge-built key agrees with them and the error stays invisible — while every number on screen looks healthy. That is the most likely way to lose this competition without noticing.

So the judges label 300 comments, and you spot-check 15. Five minutes turns the whole set from unvalidated into spot-validated, and hands the paper a real human-agreement figure.

The judge panel

Four strong models, no Google, none of them a teacher
Judge$/M inWhy it is here

Three safeguards

  1. Judges are disjoint from teachers. No model id appears in both tiers, and the code refuses to run if one does. Two families recur at very different capability points (OpenAI nano→mini, Qwen flash→plus); the working notes say so plainly rather than claiming full independence.
  2. Judge in English. Every test comment was back-translated in Stage 1, and judges see the original and the English side by side. Every model is markedly more reliable in English than in Hindi or Bengali.
  3. A second judge tries to prove the first one wrong. Only labels that survive the challenge are kept; challenged ones go to a queue rather than being guessed.
# the judges label 300 comments through the English pivot $ bash run_all.sh silver # then: open artifacts/spot_check.csv, fill `your_label` for 15 rows (~5 min)

What we can measure without human labels

Three numbers that bound reliability. None of them proves the judges are right — only a human can do that — but all three are real and all three go in the paper.

  • Agreement among the four judges (Fleiss’ κ). Independently built strong models converging on a task is evidence it is well defined.
  • Strong judges versus the cheap teacher panel. The useful one: it says how far the labels being distilled into our encoder sit from a 4× more expensive panel’s view.
  • Adversarial overturn rate — how often the challenger rejected a proposal that four strong models had already agreed on.

Reading your spot check
  • 13 or more of 15 agree — the silver set is behaving. Proceed to tuning and blending with confidence.
  • 11 or 12 — usable, but label the challenged queue in artifacts/human_queue.csv before trusting the blend weights.
  • Fewer than 11 — something is systematically off. Do not calibrate on it; the judge prompt needs re-examining first.

Whatever the number is, it goes in the paper. “We used LLM judges and measured them against human labels at 0.87” is a contribution; “we used LLM judges” is a reviewer’s objection.

The four rules, for the 15 you check

  1. Angry is not Against. “Shame on the government for this pollution” accepts the claim → Favour.
  2. Praising the video is not agreeing. “Very informative, thank you” → None.
  3. Hypocrisy cuts both ways. “They fly private jets, so it’s all drama” rejects the claim → Against. “They fly private jets, they should act first” accepts it → Favour.
  4. If you truly cannot decide, type ? — it is dropped, and we report how many were unclear.

The teacher panel’s guess is deliberately hidden from your sheet so it cannot anchor you. Comments are chosen mostly from where the panel split, because those sit on the decision boundary and teach us the most.

Stage three

Train our own model

Now we teach a compact model everything the panel knows, so we own a system that needs no API keys and can be handed to the organizers.

$ bash run_all.sh stage2

3a · One model, two jobs

The model is asked to predict two things at once: the label, and which of the 27 arguments the comment uses. The second job is the interesting one. It stops the model memorising surface words like “hoax” and pushes it to recognise the shape of the argument — which is what actually carries across languages.

Example · what the model is trained to output
Input
এই ৯৭% সংখ্যাটা কোথা থেকে এল? কেউ জানে না।
“Where did this 97% number come from? Nobody knows.”
↓ two answers
Output
Label: Against  ·  Argument: A5 · the science is unreliable

3b · Learn from the panel, then from itself

We train the model on everything at once: the English data, its Hindi and Bengali translations, the generated comments, and the real test comments labelled by the panel. Then we repeat: the model predicts the test set, we take the predictions it is most confident about, add them to its lessons, and train again.

The trap we guard against

Left alone, this loop eats its own tail: the model is least confident about Against, so it adds almost no Against examples, gets worse at Against, and spirals. We fix that two ways — new examples are admitted with a quota per label, and a prediction is rejected outright if all five panel models said something else.

After each round we print the score on your 150 hand-labelled comments. That is the only honest number, and it tells us which round to keep.

4b · Tune the decision rule — the cheapest points available

Every model outputs three numbers per comment, and normally you just take the biggest. But “take the biggest” aims at accuracy, and we are scored on macro-F1. Since Against is rare, the model is shy about it, and the honest fix is to lower the bar for that one label.

We find how far to lower it using your 150 hand-labelled comments.

Example · from the pipeline test, on a model that under-predicts Against
Before tuning
Favour 292   None 199   Against 9   →   macro-F1 0.55
Only 9 comments out of 500 called Against. That single number is capping the score.
↓ lower the bar for Against by 2.9×
After tuning
Favour 189   None 249   Against 62   →   macro-F1 0.77
Same model, same predictions underneath. Only the decision rule changed. +0.22.

Three tuning tricks are tried, and each is kept only if it actually improves the score on your labelled comments. One of them regularly makes things worse, so it gets thrown away automatically rather than quietly damaging the submission.

4c · Blend the three channels

We combine the three sets of numbers, giving each channel a weight. The weights are chosen by trying combinations and keeping whichever scores best on your 150 comments.

Pipeline test — each channel alone, versus blended
SystemMacro-F1
Our model alone, tuned0.77
AI panel alone0.61
Ready-made reasoner alone0.45

These come from a synthetic test of the plumbing, not from real data, so the absolute numbers mean nothing. What matters is the ordering: blending beats every single channel, and a plain majority vote throws away most of the benefit because it ignores how confident each model was.

Stage five

Build and check the submission

The format is unusual and easy to get wrong. Getting it wrong is a zero, so the tool refuses to produce a file unless everything checks out.

$ bash run_all.sh submit

Two files — one Hindi, one Bengali — zipped together. All three of our systems go side by side as columns in the same file, not as three separate files:

id,model1_label,model2_label,model3_label 1,Against,Against,Against 2,None,None,Favour 3,Favour,Favour,Favour … 500 rows …

ColumnSystemWhy it is included

Note the absence

The AI panel is not in this table. Everything we submit is a classifier we trained, and every column can be regenerated from a saved checkpoint with no internet connection. The panel’s contribution is already inside these models, taught to them in Stage 3.

What the checker refuses to let through
  • Any comment ID from the organizers’ file missing a prediction
  • Row count not matching the original file
  • Any label that is not exactly Favour, Against or None
  • Any blank cell

It also warns loudly if a column predicts the same label for all 500 comments — that is always a bug, and it would score near zero.

Already fixed, worth knowing

Two silent bugs that would have cost the competition

Bug 1 · the word “None” disappearing

One of our three labels is the word None. The spreadsheet library we use treats that word as “empty cell”. So every time we saved and reloaded the training data, 1,092 of 2,442 rows silently vanished — a third of the data, one whole label, and no error message anywhere. It would have looked like a two-label problem and capped the score around 0.45.

Fixed everywhere by reading files through one shared function that switches that behaviour off.

Bug 2 · the test files failing to load at all

A recent version change in the same library altered how text columns are identified. Our column detection found nothing and refused to open the real test files. Fixed, and column names are now matched regardless of capitalisation — the official files use ID and COMMENT in capitals.

Infrastructure

This machine does the thinking, a rented GPU does the training

This server is CPU-only, which is fine for everything except fine-tuning. The API work — generating, translating, the AI panel, the judges — is all network-bound and runs here happily. Only Stage 3 wants a GPU, and only for about an hour.

The one way to lose real money here

A rented instance bills every second it exists, including while doing nothing. Forget one overnight and the credit is gone. train destroys the instance when it finishes, including on failure, but if a session is ever interrupted run vast_gpu.py down — it is safe even when nothing is running — or check the instances page by eye.

The rented box also receives only the HuggingFace token, never the other thirty-odd keys. A machine you rented by the minute from a stranger is not somewhere to leave credentials.

If you remember nothing else

Five sentences

  1. The test comments are translated American comments, so translate them back to English and tell your models where they came from.
  2. The score is macro-F1, so the rare Against label is worth as much as the common ones — protect it in every single step.
  3. Submit models you trained, never a live AI call. The AI is a teacher, not a contestant; that is the pattern FIRE publishes and the one their reproducibility clause expects.
  4. Spot-check 15 of the AI's labels yourself. Unmeasured AI labels are worse than no labels, because they make you confident while being wrong — and no AI can audit its own blind spot.
  5. Never just take the biggest number. Tuning the decision rule was worth more than most modelling changes — then open the ZIP and look at it before uploading.
