\begin{abstract}
  The AISoMe 2026 track asks for the stance of Hindi and Bengali YouTube
  comments towards the claim \emph{``climate change and global warming is a
  serious concern''}, given only English training data. We report that the
  decisive step was not modelling but \emph{measurement}: inspection of the
  released evaluation files shows they are \textbf{machine-translated English}
  comments from climate-science videos---zero romanised code-mixing, zero
  emoji, two mentions of India in 500 Hindi comments, and recurring
  US-centric arguments about a science communicator's credentials and the
  ``97\,\% consensus'' figure. Building for that distribution rather than for
  organic Indic social media reversed our design. We (i) formalise a 27-node
  climate-argument taxonomy extending CARDS with an India-specific branch and
  an explicit \textsc{None} inventory, and use it as a generative schema,
  auxiliary supervision, and an annotation scaffold; (ii) distil a nine-model
  LLM panel over the unlabelled test set into compact encoders; and
  (iii) exploit the forensic finding a second time by \emph{back-translating
  the test set to English} and classifying with an English-only encoder,
  which sidesteps the cross-lingual problem instead of solving it. That last
  step is worth $+0.10$ macro-F1 over the best multilingual configuration
  ($p$aired over 300 shared splits, 100\,\% win rate). Our submitted system
  reaches macro-F1 $0.922$ (Hindi) and $0.915$ (Bengali) on a held-out
  development set. We are explicit that those development labels come from an
  LLM judge panel rather than human annotation, and we report the reliability
  evidence and its limits. We also document a methodological trap that cost us
  a near-miss: comparing ensemble strategies by \emph{unpaired} means
  overstated one gain by $+0.015$ and \emph{reversed the sign} of another.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Stance detection asks how a text positions itself towards a fixed
claim---not whether it is positive or negative, but whether it accepts or
rejects a proposition. The AISoMe 2026 shared task at FIRE poses this for
Indian-language social media: label a YouTube comment in Hindi or Bengali as
\textsc{Favour}, \textsc{Against}, or \textsc{None} with respect to
\emph{``climate change and global warming is a serious concern''}. The
organisers supply \emph{English-only} training resources and evaluate on
Hindi and Bengali, making cross-lingual transfer the nominal challenge, and
rank runs by macro-F1.

Two properties of the task shaped everything we did. First, macro-F1 weights
each class equally, so the \textsc{Against} class---about 15\,\% of the
permitted English pool---carries a full third of the score. A system that
never confidently predicts \textsc{Against} is arithmetically capped near
$0.45$ regardless of how well it does elsewhere. Second, and less obviously,
the evaluation data is not what the task description leads one to expect. We
therefore begin with a diagnostic rather than a model.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A forensic characterisation of the evaluation set}
    (\S\ref{sec:forensics}) showing it consists of machine-translated English
    comments, with the measurements that establish this and the four design
    decisions that follow from it.
  \item \textbf{A 27-node climate-argument taxonomy}
    (\S\ref{sec:taxonomy}) extending CARDS~\cite{coan2021} with an
    India-specific contrarian branch, a mirrored pro-climate branch, and an
    explicit \textsc{None} inventory; used three ways and released as a
    reusable resource.
  \item \textbf{An English-pivot classifier} (\S\ref{sec:pivot}): back-translate
    the test set and classify with an English-only encoder. Worth $+0.10$
    macro-F1 over the best multilingual system.
  \item \textbf{Transductive panel distillation} (\S\ref{sec:distil}) and
    \textbf{macro-F1-aware calibration} (\S\ref{sec:calib}), each measured
    separately.
  \item \textbf{Two negative and two methodological results}
    (\S\ref{sec:negative}) that we believe are more useful than another
    architecture: refitting on more in-distribution labels \emph{hurt};
    every ensemble was worse than the best single model; unpaired strategy
    comparison is actively misleading at this development-set size; and an
    evaluation-harness bug nearly caused us to discard our best system.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Task and Data}
\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{Evaluation data.}
Two files of 500 comments each (Hindi, Bengali), columns \texttt{ID} and
\texttt{COMMENT}, ids $1$--$500$ in both files. Runs are submitted as one file
per language with up to three classifier columns.

\paragraph{Permitted training data.}
The track links two English stance corpora: \textbf{GWSD}~\cite{luo2020},
2\,300 news sentences each annotated by eight crowd workers
(agree/disagree/neutral); and \textbf{SemEval-2016 Task 6}~\cite{mohammad2016},
restricted to the target \emph{``Climate Change is a Real Concern''}
(395 tweets). Other public data, machine translation and synthetic generation
are explicitly permitted, and the organisers encourage making the training set
``more appropriate for the task''.

We merge both sources into 2\,442 rows after de-duplication
(\textsc{None} 1\,092 / \textsc{Favour} 983 / \textsc{Against} 367). For GWSD we
retain the \emph{full crowd vote distribution} rather than only the majority
label: an 8--0 vote and a 5--3 vote are very different training signals, and the
soft target is a free regulariser for the small \textsc{Against} class. Mean
GWSD annotator agreement is $0.72$.

\paragraph{The class-balance problem.}
Only $15.0\,\%$ of the pool is \textsc{Against}, and within SemEval-CC the rate
is $3.8\,\%$ (15 of 395). Under macro-F1 this is not a detail; it is the task.

The register is uniformly formal, and the arguments are US-centric: attacks on
a presenter's credentials (\emph{``not a scientist, a mechanical engineer''}),
disputes over the ``97\,\%'' consensus figure, channel-merchandise spam,
Abrahamic creationist framing (\emph{``why did God kill the dinosaurs''}), and
school-assignment comments (\emph{``who else is here for online classes''}).
Numerals appear in Devanagari and Bengali digits (\foreignlanguage{english}{९७\%}),
and English names are transliterated. We conclude the evaluation set consists of
\textbf{English YouTube comments machine-translated into Hindi and Bengali}.

Number-signature matching (the multiset of numerals per comment, normalised
across Devanagari, Bengali and ASCII digits) shows the two language files are
\emph{different} comment samples rather than translations of the same 500
English comments, so no cross-file alignment exploit exists.

\paragraph{Four consequences.}
This reverses the natural design.
(1) \emph{Translate-test becomes the primary channel, not a fallback}:
back-translating recovers near-original English, the native domain of GWSD,
SemEval and every strong English encoder (\S\ref{sec:pivot}).
(2) \emph{Provenance context helps annotation}: one sentence telling an LLM
annotator that these are translated comments from a climate-science video
resolves whole families of otherwise-ambiguous items.
(3) \emph{Synthetic data must imitate translationese}, not authentic Indian
slang---pure native script, formal register, no emoji, transliterated names.
(4) \emph{The argument inventory to cover is the source community's}, not the
target language's. Our India-specific taxonomy branch, designed before this
measurement, became a minor slice rather than the centrepiece; we report it as
an ablation and treat the reframing as the finding.

\section{The Nirnay Argument Taxonomy}
\label{sec:taxonomy}

Cross-lingual stance transfer for climate discourse fails less because of
\emph{language} mismatch than because of \emph{argument-inventory} mismatch.
GWSD and SemEval encode a US contrarian idiom; translating them yields fluent
Hindi sentences about Al Gore, which is grammatically perfect and
pedagogically useless. We therefore pivot through the argument.

Our taxonomy has \textbf{27 nodes} (13 \textsc{Against}, 7 \textsc{Favour},
7 \textsc{None}) with 228 cue phrases across English, Hindi and Bengali:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{CARDS branch} (6 nodes), after Coan et al.~\cite{coan2021}:
    not happening; not human-caused; impacts not bad; solutions will not work;
    science unreliable; and one we added, \emph{attacks on the messenger},
    which the forensics showed to be among the most frequent arguments present.
  \item \textbf{India-specific contrarian branch} (7 nodes): climate
    colonialism and whataboutism; development-first; local-weather naturalism;
    cyclical or religious cosmology; media/TRP conspiracy; elite hypocrisy;
    population-not-climate.
  \item \textbf{Pro-climate branch} (7 nodes) grounded in lived experience:
    floods and air quality; agricultural harm; health; intergenerational duty;
    demands for action; support for mitigation; trust in science.
  \item \textbf{\textsc{None} inventory} (7 nodes): channel praise; pure
    questions; neutral facts; unrelated politics; spam; ambivalence; and
    \emph{class-assignment} comments, again added after the forensics.
\end{itemize}

Two annotation traps are encoded explicitly, because they are where systems and
annotators disagree most:
\emph{sentiment is not stance}---\emph{``Shame on this government, they have
destroyed our rivers!''} is \textsc{Favour} despite maximal negative
sentiment---and \emph{praise of the video is not agreement with the
claim}---\emph{``very informative, thank you''} is \textsc{None} even under a
climate-alarm video.

The taxonomy is used three ways, and that triple use is what makes it a
contribution rather than a lexicon appendix: as a \emph{generative schema}
(\S\ref{sec:synth}), as \emph{auxiliary supervision} (\S\ref{sec:encoder}), and
as the \emph{reasoning scaffold} in the LLM annotation prompt
(\S\ref{sec:distil}).

\section{Method}

\subsection{Taxonomy-conditioned synthetic augmentation}
\label{sec:synth}

We walk the product
$\text{node} \times \text{language} \times \text{script} \times \text{register}$
and prompt an LLM for realistic comments per cell. Because the stance is fixed
by the cell, the corpus is \textbf{class-balanced by construction}. Following
\S\ref{sec:forensics} we generate in \emph{translationese} style. The result is
1\,040 comments covering all 27 nodes, $48\,\%$ \textsc{Against} against
$15\,\%$ in the real pool, with provenance columns
(node, branch, language, register, generator) retained for ablation.
Near-duplicates are removed by token Jaccard within (node, language).

\subsection{Transductive panel distillation}
\label{sec:distil}

The entire \emph{unlabelled} evaluation set is available at inference time.
Using it is transduction, not leakage: no organiser-provided label is ever read.
We run two disjoint LLM panels over all 1\,000 comments.

A \textbf{teacher panel} of five cheap models from five different laboratories
labels every comment, each required to name a taxonomy node \emph{before}
emitting a stance---chain-of-thought with a fixed, auditable vocabulary. A
\textbf{judge panel} of three stronger models then adjudicates, reading the
Indic original \emph{and} its English back-translation together, with a fourth
model adversarially challenging every proposed label.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{Panel reliability. The judge panel is disjoint from the teacher panel
by construction and the code refuses to run if any model id appears in both.}
\label{tab:panels}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
Measure & Value \\
\midrule
Teacher panel Fleiss' $\kappa$ (5 models) & 0.767 / 0.749 \\
Judge panel Fleiss' $\kappa$ (3 models) & \textbf{0.859} \\
Judge labels accepted (of 1\,000) & 971 \\
Adversarial overturn rate & 2.8\,\% \\
Teacher vs.\ judge agreement & 92.7\,\% \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The 971 accepted judge labels are split stratified by
(language $\times$ label) into \textbf{727 distillation rows} and a
\textbf{244-row held-out development set}, with a hard assertion that the two
share no (language, id) key. Every number in \S\ref{sec:results} is measured on
that held-out set.

\subsection{Argument-aware encoder}
\label{sec:encoder}

Encoders carry two heads over a shared representation: a 3-way stance head
trained with class-weighted cross-entropy plus a soft-label KL term, and an
auxiliary 28-way \emph{argument-node} head with weight $\lambda = 0.3$. The
auxiliary signal is free---synthetic rows are node-labelled by construction and
the panels predict a node for every real comment---and it pushes the shared
representation towards argument structure rather than surface lexical cues,
which is the axis that transfers. Rows without a node are masked out of the
auxiliary loss. We use class weighting rather than oversampling because
duplicating rows distorts soft targets whereas reweighting does not.

\subsection{The English pivot}
\label{sec:pivot}

This is the component that mattered most, and it follows directly from
\S\ref{sec:forensics}. We back-translate all 1\,000 evaluation comments to
English and fine-tune \textbf{DeBERTa-v3-large}~\cite{he2021} on an
English-only pool: the 2\,442 GWSD/SemEval rows plus the back-translated
judge-labelled test rows. Rather than asking a multilingual encoder to bridge
English training and Indic evaluation, we move the evaluation to where the
training already lives.

\subsection{Macro-F1-aware calibration}
\label{sec:calib}

$\arg\max p(y \mid x)$ maximises accuracy, not macro-F1. We apply, in order,
temperature scaling~\cite{guo2017}, prior-shift correction by the
Saerens--Latinne--Decaestecker EM procedure~\cite{saerens2002}, and a
coordinate search over per-class multiplicative weights. Crucially each stage is
\textbf{accepted greedily}---kept only if it does not lower development
macro-F1. This is not a formality: EM prior correction on Bengali would have
taken one channel from $0.781$ to $0.200$ and was rejected automatically. We
also floor and damp the EM update, because on an under-confident model the
textbook procedure drives $p(\textsc{Against})$ to zero and removes the class
entirely.

\section{Experimental Setup}

Backbones: MuRIL base/large~\cite{khanuja2021}, XLM-R base/large~\cite{conneau2020},
IndicBERTv2~\cite{doddapaneni2023}, DeBERTa-v3-large~\cite{he2021}, and LaBSE~\cite{feng2022}
as a frozen encoder. Max length 128, seed 20260502. Base models were trained on
32 CPU cores; large models on a single rented RTX 4090 (bf16, flash-attention
2.7.4 where the architecture implements it, otherwise PyTorch SDPA), at a total
compute cost of USD 1.11. All three submitted runs are locally reproducible
checkpoints with no API call at inference.

We also evaluate a \textbf{projection channel} adapting the hybrid framework of
Deb et al.~\cite{deb2026}: LaBSE embeddings are projected into a semantic space
built from the taxonomy's gloss-and-cue embeddings by a KAN\,+\,SVR
hybrid~\cite{liu2024}, and stance is read off cosine similarity aggregated over
nodes. Here the taxonomy replaces ConceptNet as the knowledge source, which is
both domain-matched and roughly nine times richer than a bare 3-way label space.

Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the main result. The English pivot dominates every
multilingual configuration by a wide margin. Paired over 300 shared half-splits
against the best pre-pivot system, the calibrated pivot gains
$+0.099$ (Hindi, $50.2$ SE, 100\,\% of splits) and $+0.115$ (Bengali,
$58.9$ SE, 100\,\%).

Before adopting a gain that large we verified it was not leakage: the English
distillation pool shares \emph{zero} (language, id) keys with the development
set, \emph{zero} development comment texts appear verbatim in any training text,
and the GWSD/SemEval pool contains no evaluation comments at all.

\paragraph{Component contributions.}
Calibration is worth $+0.019$ to $+0.023$ on the pivot channel and $+0.04$ to
$+0.08$ on base encoders. Model scale is worth $+0.05$ to $+0.08$
(MuRIL-base $\rightarrow$ large; XLM-R-base $0.717 \rightarrow$ large $0.836$).
The auxiliary node head and the soft-label term are both retained in the
submitted configuration.

\paragraph{Submitted runs.}
Both language files carry
\texttt{model1} $=$ calibrated English pivot,
\texttt{model2} $=$ English-pool DeBERTa variant,
\texttt{model3} $=$ XLM-R-large on native script. All three are trained
classifiers; the three columns agree on $87.2\,\%$ (Hindi) and $86.4\,\%$
(Bengali) of comments.

\section{Negative and Methodological Results}
\label{sec:negative}

\paragraph{More in-distribution labels hurt.}
Refitting the distilled encoder on all 971 judge labels rather than the 727 that
exclude the development set made it \emph{worse} (hi $0.655$, bn $0.684$ versus
$0.758$/$0.781$), despite then training on the very rows it was scored against.
The likely cause is checkpoint selection: with an internal early-stopping split
drawn from a pool that is $92\,\%$ synthetic and translated text, ``best epoch''
is chosen against the wrong distribution. More data is not automatically better
when it changes what you early-stop on.

\paragraph{Every ensemble lost to the best single model.}
Against the calibrated pivot, all tested blends were worse
($-0.009$ to $-0.035$; most won $0\,\%$ of 300 splits). With $\approx$120
development rows per language, combination weights cannot be estimated
reliably, and a strong single model is the safer choice.

\paragraph{Unpaired strategy comparison is misleading.}
Our strategy selector ranked configurations by mean held-out macro-F1 over
independently drawn half-splits. On identical splits, one apparent Hindi gain of
$+0.015$ shrank to $+0.005$, and an apparent Bengali gain of $+0.023$
\emph{reversed sign} to $-0.002$. Any ensembling decision at this data size must
be made on paired differences over shared splits.

\paragraph{An evaluation-harness bug nearly cost us the best system.}
Our trainer's in-training development score for the English pivot read $0.574$,
and a retry read $0.331$, so we briefly abandoned the idea. Both figures were
artefacts: the harness evaluated using a text column that held the \emph{Indic}
original, so an English-only model was being scored on Devanagari. The
prediction path had been correct all along. We report this because the failure
mode---a silent column mismatch producing a plausible-looking low score---is
easy to repeat and hard to notice.

\section{Error Analysis}

The best system's weakest class is \textsc{None} in both languages
($0.870$ hi, $0.863$ bn versus $\geq 0.92$ for \textsc{Against}). This matches
where the taxonomy predicted difficulty: \textsc{None} is not a semantic
category but the absence of a stance, and it collects channel praise,
questions, neutral facts and ambivalence---categories whose boundary with
\textsc{Favour} depends on a codebook decision rather than on the text. The
most frequent confusions are \textsc{None}\,$\rightarrow$\,\textsc{Favour}
(polite engagement read as agreement) and
\textsc{Against}\,$\rightarrow$\,\textsc{Favour} (sarcasm surviving translation
poorly).

The most frequent arguments the panels assigned were
\emph{demands for action} (D5), \emph{science unreliable} (A5),
\emph{attacks on the messenger} (A6) and \emph{channel praise} (N1). That A6
ranks fourth in Bengali is a direct vindication of adding it after the
forensics.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Development labels are not human gold.}
This is the most important caveat. Our 244 development labels come from the
LLM judge panel, so every figure in Table~\ref{tab:main} measures
\emph{agreement with that panel}, not with ground truth. The panel's internal
agreement is high ($\kappa = 0.859$) and a human reader reviewed and endorsed a
15-comment sample, but that review was \emph{not} a blind annotation and
therefore yields no independent agreement statistic. If the panel's reading of
the \textsc{None} boundary differs systematically from the organisers', our
official score will be lower than reported, and our calibration---fitted to the
panel's class priors---may transfer poorly. We regard closing this gap with a
blind human annotation of 50--150 comments as the single highest-value
extension of this work.

\paragraph{The pivot requires machine translation at inference.}
\texttt{model1} consumes back-translated English. The classifier is a local
checkpoint with no API dependency, but reproducing the run requires either the
back-translated files (which we release) or a re-translation step. Machine
translation is standard and organiser-encouraged for this task, but it is a
pipeline dependency and we state it plainly.

\paragraph{Synthetic data reflects LLM priors.}
The 1\,040 generated comments encode a language model's \emph{model} of Indian
climate discourse, which is not the same thing as that discourse.

\paragraph{Small development set.}
$\approx$120 rows per language; all ensembling conclusions are correspondingly
weak, which is itself one of our findings.

%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}

The lesson we would carry to another cross-lingual shared task is procedural:
\emph{measure the evaluation distribution before choosing a transfer strategy}.
Here the nominal problem was Hindi and Bengali social media; the actual problem
was translated English, and recognising that was worth more than every
architectural choice we made combined. The argument taxonomy, the synthetic
corpus and the judge labels are released so that the pieces can be reused and,
where we were wrong, corrected.
