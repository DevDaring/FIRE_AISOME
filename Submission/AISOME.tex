\begin{abstract}
  Public discussion of climate change on Indian video platforms is active,
  but labelled stance data in Indian languages is scarce. This paper
  describes our submission to the AISoMe 2026 shared task at FIRE 2026:
  given only English training resources, assign \textsc{Favour},
  \textsc{Against} or \textsc{None} to Hindi and Bengali YouTube comments on
  the claim \emph{``climate change and global warming is a serious
  concern''}. Before training any model, we examined the 1\,000 evaluation
  comments and found strong evidence that they are machine-translated from
  English: they contain no code-mixing or emoji and refer to a United States
  science communicator rather than to India. This diagnosis motivated our
  system design. We built a 27-node taxonomy of
  climate arguments and used it to generate a balanced synthetic corpus, to
  supervise an auxiliary classification head, and to structure the prompts of
  a nine-model language-model panel (five teachers, three judges and one
  adversarial challenger), whose labels on the unlabelled
  evaluation set we distilled into compact encoders. The primary run
  translates the evaluation comments back into English and classifies them
  with an English-only encoder, which improves macro-F1 by about $0.10$ over
  the best multilingual-encoder system, averaged over 300 paired half-splits
  of the development set. The submitted
  system reaches macro-F1 $0.922$ (Hindi) and $0.915$ (Bengali) against
  held-out judge-panel labels (123 Hindi and 121 Bengali comments). Because these development labels are
  model-generated rather than human gold, we report their reliability
  evidence and discuss this limitation explicitly.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Stance detection asks how a text positions itself with respect to a fixed
claim. It is not sentiment: a furious comment and an approving one can take
the same stance, and a polite one can take none at all. In the AISoMe 2026
shared task at FIRE 2026, the text is a YouTube comment in Hindi or Bengali
and the claim is \emph{``climate change and global warming is a serious
concern''}. The organisers supply English-only training resources and
evaluate on the two Indian languages, so cross-lingual transfer is the
nominal challenge. Runs are ranked by macro-F1, and more than eighty runs
were submitted to the track.

Two properties of the task shaped our approach. First, macro-F1 weights
every class equally. The \textsc{Against} class makes up only $15\,\%$ of
the permitted English pool, yet it carries a full third of the score: a
system that never predicts \textsc{Against} confidently forfeits that
class's F1 entirely, and even perfect \textsc{Favour} and \textsc{None}
predictions then cap macro-F1 at $0.67$. Second, and less obviously, the
evaluation data is not what the task description leads one to expect. We
therefore begin with a diagnostic rather than a model
(Section~\ref{sec:forensics}).

Our novel contribution is a diagnose-first transfer strategy. We document
linguistic and topical evidence that the nominally Hindi and Bengali
evaluation set was machine-translated from English YouTube comments, then
use this diagnosis to motivate back-translation and source-language
classification. This English pivot
improves macro-F1 by about $0.10$ over the best multilingual-encoder system
(XLM-R-large, Table~\ref{tab:main}), averaged over 300 paired half-splits of
the held-out judge-panel development set. The taxonomy, synthetic augmentation,
panel distillation, and calibration support this strategy rather than
constituting separate novelty claims.

\section{Related Work}

Stance detection was established as a benchmark task by SemEval-2016 Task
6~\cite{mohammad2016} and extended to unseen targets by zero-shot
formulations~\cite{allaway2020}. For climate discourse specifically, GWSD
provides English news sentences with crowd stance annotations~\cite{luo2020}.
The CARDS scheme~\cite{coan2021} organises contrarian climate claims into a
hierarchy of super-claims and sub-claims; our taxonomy adopts it as one
branch.

Cross-lingual transfer for Indian languages typically relies on multilingual
encoders such as XLM-R~\cite{conneau2020}, MuRIL~\cite{khanuja2021} and
IndicBERTv2~\cite{doddapaneni2023}. An alternative reformulates
classification as entailment against a hypothesis~\cite{yin2019}, and
XNLI-trained models~\cite{conneau2018xnli} make this available
multilingually; we include it as a baseline. Language models are
increasingly applied to stance detection directly~\cite{pangtey2025}. We use
them as teachers instead, following knowledge distillation~\cite{hinton2015}:
the panel labels unlabelled target data, and a compact encoder learns from
those labels, so the submitted run is a local classifier rather than a live
API call. Our projection channel adapts a hybrid framework for multilingual
zero-shot classification~\cite{deb2026}; where that work aligns sentence
embeddings with ConceptNet~\cite{speer2017} label embeddings, we substitute
the task-specific taxonomy, and we retain its Kolmogorov--Arnold
alignment~\cite{liu2025kan}.

\section{Task and Data}
\label{sec:data}

We first describe the data as released by the organisers, because every
later design decision depends on it. The evaluation data comprises two files
of 500 comments each, for Hindi and Bengali, with columns \texttt{ID} and
\texttt{COMMENT} and identifiers $1$--$500$ in both files. Runs are
submitted as one file per language carrying up to three classifier columns.

The permitted training resources are two English stance corpora:
GWSD~\cite{luo2020}, 2\,300 news sentences each annotated by eight crowd
workers, and SemEval-2016 Task 6~\cite{mohammad2016} restricted to the
target \emph{``Climate Change is a Real Concern''} (395 tweets). Other
public data, machine translation and synthetic generation are explicitly
permitted.

Merging and de-duplicating the two corpora yields 2\,442 rows;
Figure~\ref{fig:shift} shows their class composition. Two details matter for
how we train. For GWSD we retain the full crowd-vote distribution rather
than only the majority label: an 8--0 vote and a 5--3 vote are different
training signals, and the soft target regularises the small
\textsc{Against} class at no cost. On average, $72\,\%$ of a GWSD
sentence's eight crowd workers chose its majority label.
The pool is also strongly skewed for a macro-F1 task: within the SemEval
subset only 15 tweets of 395 are \textsc{Against}.

\section{Characterising the Evaluation Set}
\label{sec:forensics}

Before training any model we measured the released files
(Table~\ref{tab:forensics}). Organic Indian YouTube comments are short,
emoji-dense and substantially Roman-script. These comments are none of those
things.

\midrule
Comments naming one science communicator & 63 & 96 \\
Roman-script code-mixing & 0 & 0 \\
Comments containing any emoji & 0 & 0 \\
Comments mentioning India & 2 & 3 \\
Median length (characters) & 146 & 147 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

The register is uniformly formal. The arguments come from a United
States-centric repertoire: attacks on a presenter's credentials
(\emph{``not a scientist, a mechanical engineer''}), disputes over the
``97\,\%'' consensus figure, channel-merchandise spam, creationist framing
(\emph{``why did God kill the dinosaurs''}), and comments indicating a
school assignment (\emph{``who else is here for online classes''}). Numerals
appear in Devanagari and Bengali digits, and English names are
transliterated. Taken together, these indicators are consistent with English
YouTube comments machine-translated into Hindi and Bengali; we treat this as
an evidence-based characterisation, not as a claim of recovered provenance.
Table~\ref{tab:examples} shows four back-translated examples.

The two language files are different comment samples, not translations of
the same 500 English comments. Matching the multiset of numerals per
comment, normalised across Devanagari, Bengali and ASCII digits, recovers no
cross-file alignment, so no alignment shortcut is available.

This diagnosis guides the system design in four ways. First, translate-test
becomes the primary channel rather than a fallback: back-translation recovers
English text in the domain of GWSD, SemEval, and strong English encoders
(Section~\ref{sec:pivot}). Second, telling the annotation models in one
prompt sentence that the comments are machine-translated from English
YouTube comments under climate-science videos resolves whole families of
otherwise ambiguous items (channel praise, presenter attacks, assignment
chatter). Third, synthetic data should imitate
translationese---the literal, formal register of machine-translated
text---rather than authentic Indian slang. Fourth, the argument inventory to
cover is the source community's, not the target language's. Our
India-specific taxonomy branch was designed before this measurement; it
accordingly became a minor slice, and we report it as an ablation rather
than as the centrepiece.

\section{Argument Taxonomy}
\label{sec:taxonomy}

Cross-lingual stance transfer can also fail through mismatch in the argument
inventory. GWSD and SemEval encode a United States contrarian idiom, so their
translations do not necessarily cover the arguments encountered in the
target data. The taxonomy therefore represents arguments alongside language.

The taxonomy contains 27 nodes---13 \textsc{Against}, 7 \textsc{Favour} and
7 \textsc{None}---with 228 cue phrases across English, Hindi and Bengali.
The CARDS branch follows Coan et al.~\cite{coan2021}: not happening; not
human-caused; impacts not harmful; solutions will not work; science
unreliable; and \emph{attacks on the messenger}, which we added after the
measurements of Section~\ref{sec:forensics} showed it to be among the most
frequent arguments actually present. The India-specific contrarian branch
covers climate colonialism and whataboutism, development-first arguments,
local-weather naturalism, cyclical or religious cosmology, media conspiracy,
elite hypocrisy and population-rather-than-climate. The pro-climate branch
is grounded in lived experience: floods and air quality, agricultural harm,
health, intergenerational duty, demands for action, support for mitigation
and trust in science. The \textsc{None} inventory covers channel praise,
pure questions, neutral factual statements, unrelated politics, spam,
ambivalence and---also added after the measurements---comments indicating a
class assignment.

Two annotation traps are encoded explicitly, because they are where systems
and annotators most often diverge. Sentiment is not stance: \emph{``Shame on
this government, they have destroyed our rivers!''} is \textsc{Favour}
despite maximally negative sentiment. Praise of the video is not agreement
with the claim: \emph{``very informative, thank you''} is \textsc{None} even
under a climate-alarm video.

The taxonomy is used in three roles: as a generative schema
(Section~\ref{sec:synth}), as auxiliary supervision
(Section~\ref{sec:encoder}), and as the reasoning scaffold in the annotation
prompt (Section~\ref{sec:distil}). That triple use is what makes it a
resource rather than a lexicon appendix.

\subsection{Taxonomy-conditioned synthetic augmentation}
\label{sec:synth}

We walk the product $\text{node} \times \text{language} \times
\text{register}$ and prompt a language model for realistic comments in each
cell. The stance of a comment is fixed by its cell, so the corpus is
class-balanced by construction. Following Section~\ref{sec:forensics} we
generate in translationese style. The result is 1\,040 comments covering all
27 nodes. Provenance columns (node, branch, language, register, generator)
are retained for ablation, and near-duplicates are removed by token Jaccard
similarity within each (node, language) cell.

Figure~\ref{fig:shift} provides a descriptive distributional check. We built
the synthetic corpus to be \textsc{Against}-heavy from the forensic reading
before producing any panel labels. The judge-panel labels on the evaluation
set later showed an \textsc{Against} share only four percentage points away
from the synthetic corpus's. This is not
an independent evaluation, because the comparison uses model-generated
labels, but it supports the motivation for the synthetic class balance.

\subsection{Transductive panel distillation}
\label{sec:distil}

We use the entire \emph{unlabelled} evaluation set for target-adaptive
pseudo-labelling; no organiser-provided label is accessed. Two disjoint
panels label all 1\,000 comments;
Table~\ref{tab:panels} summarises their reliability.

A teacher panel of five inexpensive models from five different laboratories
labels every comment. Each model must name a taxonomy node \emph{before}
emitting a stance, which constrains its reasoning to a fixed, auditable
vocabulary. A judge panel of three stronger models then adjudicates, reading
the Indic original together with its English back-translation, while a
fourth model adversarially challenges every proposed label. The two panels
are disjoint by construction, and the code refuses to run if any model
identifier appears in both roles.

The accepted labels are split, stratified by language and label, into 727
distillation rows and a 244-row held-out development set, with a
programmatic check that the two share no (language, identifier) key. Every result in
Section~\ref{sec:results} is measured against this held-out judge-panel
split, not against organiser-provided gold labels.

\subsection{Argument-aware encoder}
\label{sec:encoder}

Each encoder carries two heads over a shared representation: a three-way
stance head, trained with class-weighted cross-entropy plus a soft-label KL
term, and an auxiliary argument-node head of weight $\lambda = 0.3$. The
auxiliary signal is free, because synthetic rows are node-labelled by
construction and the panels predict a node for every real comment. It pushes
the shared representation towards argument structure rather than surface
lexical cues, which is the axis along which we need the model to generalise.
Rows without a node are masked out of the auxiliary loss. We use class
weighting rather than oversampling, because duplicating rows distorts soft
targets whereas reweighting does not.

\subsection{The English pivot}
\label{sec:pivot}

The largest gain comes from the component that follows most directly from
Section~\ref{sec:forensics}. We back-translate all 1\,000 evaluation
comments into English and fine-tune DeBERTa-v3-large~\cite{he2023} on an
English-only pool: the 2\,442 GWSD and SemEval rows plus the back-translated
judge-labelled rows. Instead of asking a multilingual encoder to bridge
English training data and Indic evaluation data, we move the evaluation data
to where the training data already lives.

\subsection{Macro-F1-aware calibration}
\label{sec:calib}

Taking $\arg\max_y p(y \mid x)$ maximises accuracy, not macro-F1. We
therefore apply three post-hoc stages in order: temperature
scaling~\cite{guo2017}, prior-shift correction by the
Saerens--Latinne--Decaestecker expectation-maximisation
procedure~\cite{saerens2002}, and a coordinate search over per-class
multiplicative weights. Each stage is kept only if it does not lower
development macro-F1. The greedy acceptance matters: on Bengali, prior
correction would have reduced the distilled MuRIL-base channel from $0.781$
to $0.200$, and it was rejected automatically. We also floor and damp the
expectation-maximisation update, because on an under-confident model the
textbook procedure drives $p(\textsc{Against})$ to zero and removes the
class entirely.

\section{Experimental Setup}

This section collects the details needed to reproduce the runs. We evaluate
MuRIL base and large~\cite{khanuja2021}, XLM-R base and
large~\cite{conneau2020}, IndicBERTv2~\cite{doddapaneni2023},
DeBERTa-v3-large~\cite{he2023}, and LaBSE~\cite{feng2022} as a frozen
encoder. The maximum sequence length is 128 and the random seed is fixed
throughout. Base models were trained on 32 CPU cores and large models on a
single rented RTX 4090 in bfloat16, at a total rental cost of about one US
dollar. All submitted runs are local checkpoints and require no API call at
inference.

We also evaluate a projection channel that adapts the hybrid framework of
Deb et al.~\cite{deb2026}. LaBSE embeddings are projected into a semantic
space built from the taxonomy's gloss-and-cue embeddings by a hybrid of a
Kolmogorov--Arnold network~\cite{liu2025kan} and support-vector regression,
and stance is read from cosine similarity aggregated over nodes. The
taxonomy here replaces ConceptNet~\cite{speer2017} as the knowledge source:
it is domain-matched and much richer than a bare three-way label space.
Finally we include a training-free entailment baseline~\cite{yin2019} using
a multilingual XNLI model~\cite{conneau2018xnli}.

informative, and Table~\ref{tab:testdist} gives the label distribution each
run assigned to the evaluation set. All three runs place \textsc{Against}
first and \textsc{None} last in both languages, which corroborates from the
model side the class balance we inferred when characterising the evaluation
files in Section~\ref{sec:forensics}: the evaluation set is far richer in
\textsc{Against} than the permitted English training pool, whose
\textsc{Against} share is $15\,\%$. The three runs also disagree in a
structured way. Moving from the pivot to the native-script encoder moves mass
out of \textsc{Favour}, which falls by $3.8$ points in Hindi and $6.6$ in
Bengali, and into \textsc{None}, which rises by $3.4$ and $3.6$; this is the
same \textsc{Favour}/\textsc{None} confusion that dominates our development
error analysis.

\section{Error Analysis}

The submitted system's weakest class is \textsc{None} in both languages on
the panel-labelled development split (Table~\ref{tab:main}). This matches where the taxonomy predicted
difficulty. \textsc{None} is not a semantic category but the absence of a
stance: it collects channel praise, questions, neutral facts and
ambivalence, whose boundary with \textsc{Favour} rests on a codebook
decision rather than on the text. The most frequent confusions are
\textsc{None} predicted as \textsc{Favour}, where polite engagement is read
as agreement, and \textsc{Against} predicted as \textsc{Favour}, where
sarcasm survives translation poorly.

Across all 1\,000 comments, the most frequent arguments assigned by the
panels are demands for action and science-unreliable in both languages;
channel praise ranks third in Bengali and fourth in Hindi, and attacks on
the messenger rank fourth in Bengali, which supports the decision to add
that node after the measurements of Section~\ref{sec:forensics}.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The most important caveat is that our development labels are not human gold.
The 244 development labels come from the judge panel, so every figure in
Table~\ref{tab:main} measures agreement with that panel rather than with
ground truth. The panel's internal agreement is high
(Table~\ref{tab:panels}), and a human reader reviewed and endorsed a
fifteen-comment sample of the development set, but that review was not blind
and yields no independent agreement statistic. If the panel's reading of the
\textsc{None} boundary differs systematically from the organisers', our
official score will be lower than reported here, and our calibration, fitted
to the panel's class priors, may transfer imperfectly. We regard a blind
human annotation of 50--150 comments as the single highest-value extension
of this work.

The pivot also requires machine translation at inference. The classifier
itself is a local checkpoint with no API dependency, but reproducing the run
requires either the back-translated files, which we release with this paper,
or a re-translation step. Machine translation is a standard and
organiser-encouraged step for this task, but it remains a pipeline
dependency. Finally, the synthetic corpus reflects a language
model's \emph{model} of Indian climate discourse rather than the discourse
itself, and the development set is small, at roughly 120 rows per language,
so ensemble-level comparisons would be unreliable at this size.

%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}

The lesson we would carry to another cross-lingual shared task is
procedural: characterise the evaluation distribution before choosing a
transfer strategy. Here the nominal problem was Hindi and Bengali social
media, while the released comments showed strong evidence of translated
English provenance. Recognising that distinction motivated the English-pivot
strategy. The argument taxonomy, synthetic corpus, and panel labels are
released with this paper so that the components can be reused and examined
further.
